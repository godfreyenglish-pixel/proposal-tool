Step 1 · Why it exists

The grid is cheap at night and expensive by day.

Electricity is made and delivered in real time. Overnight, demand is low and power is cheap. During the day and evening, everyone's running at once — the grid strains, and that power costs far more to deliver. A flat rate hides this by charging you one blended price. Time-of-use pricing simply makes the real cost visible: cheap off-peak, pricey on-peak.

Step 2 · Why Con Ed wants you on it

They want to flatten the peak.
The 16-hour window is the catch.

Con Ed benefits when customers shift usage off the strained daytime grid — it delays billions in grid upgrades. That's why they're steering homes onto the time-of-use rate (often called Rate III). But because their peak window is a punishing 16 hours long, simply switching your rate usually means paying the higher peak price for most of your day.

So switching to time-of-use on its own is a coin flip that usually loses. The rate switch creates the exposure. Something has to cover the 16-hour peak. That's where the battery comes in.

Step 3 · The strategy

Charge cheap. Run on it all day.

A home battery charges itself overnight while power is cheap, then powers your house through the entire expensive peak window. You buy low and use it during high — every single day. That daily spread, captured automatically, is the engine of the savings.

Step 4 · Compared to traditional solar

A different kind of savings — and it stacks.

Rooftop solar is a good product. But in New York it's a long game: a typical system runs ~8–12 years to pay back, you still pay delivery charges, and exported power is credited at a lower value than you pay. Battery arbitrage earns from a spread that repeats daily, captures its own incentives, and keeps your lights on in an outage — a benefit solar alone can't give you.

Step 5 · Why the timing matters

Con Ed's rates are going up. Your spread goes up with them.

In January 2026 New York regulators approved multi-year Con Edison increases — roughly 3% a year through 2028. As the peak price climbs, the gap between cheap overnight power and expensive daytime power gets wider — and a battery that arbitrages that gap earns more every year. Locking in now means every future rate hike works in your favor instead of against you.
